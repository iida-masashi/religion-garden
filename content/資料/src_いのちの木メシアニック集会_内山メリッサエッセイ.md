For more than a decade, I felt like the only Jew in my Tokyo neighborhood. I moved here 17 years ago with my Japanese husband, and it's been lonely at times. Our kids are the only Jewish students at their school. The only ones in our neighborhood walking to Tashlich at a Japanese river. The only ones breaking the fast on Yom Kippur.

And then, one night in 2019, four Israelis—introduced to us by mutual friends —turned up at our door. Students in Hebrew University’s Asian Studies Department, they had traveled to Japan for Rosh Hashanah. As an American Jew who doesn’t speak Hebrew, I felt shy around them. But here they were, in the country that I shared with my husband and my Japanese-Jewish children.

The only picture I snapped that day shows my eight-year-olddaughter reaching for the glazed round challah on the Oneg table. They stayed for a week, and we all became close. They were gracious, ambitious. One of the students—Yaron Lischinsky— told my husband about his hopes to be a light for his nation and for G-d.

I had never come across Israeli tourists, or heard Hebrew spoken in the street. Yet I had recently learned that, each year, tens of thousands of Israelis enter Japan on their Israeli passports. (And how many more Israelis fly with an alternative passport.)

“You could host them!” our new friends suggested. “You could run a guest house!” As soon as it was in the air, the idea lodged in our hearts and grew. During Covid, we sold our narrow Tokyo home and moved into a larger house. We began to renovate—turning it into a home for ourselves, my parents-in-law, and our guests.

Since then, my family and I have hosted over 2,000 remarkable young Israelis. We have never advertised our guesthouse, but our visitors keep coming, all through word of mouth.

They are dancers, pilots, paramedics, scientists, chefs, musicians, artists and chefs. Their families are the survivors of pogroms, gas chambers, death marches, trauma. They are the survivors of October 7, of months of war in Gaza, of rockets landing near their feet and cars. They are young people using rudimentary Japanese to build cultural bridges; they are ambassadors for Israel. And, like me, they are often the first Jew a Japanese person has ever met.

I had no idea that we would bond so deeply with our guests, sobbing and rejoicing together. They have become family. Yaron never could have known just how influential this home would become for us and our children.

Since October 7, it has all been more tragic, and more complicated. I am tired of telling my son bad news. He senses it even before I speak: *Is it Guy? Hamas?* His curls spring up as he hugs me, shoulders solid.

Thanks to the friendships they have formed with our Israeli guests, my children have become proud Zionists. Recently, my 12-year-old son strode through LAX airport in his IDF hat and his Magen David necklace, singing “Am Yisrael Chai.” When his older sister nudged him—“*Mom is anxious when we travel!*”—he only sang louder, face set, chin high.

My kids know that Guy Gilboa-Dalal had already bought his ticket to Japan when he attended the Nova festival. They wear the shirts we made with Japanese print on the bottom—phonetic Hebrew for “come home soon.” My son writes a two-page school report in perfect Japanese about October 7 and Hamas. He yells at our Japanese TV with Middle East coverage delivered by Al Jazeera. Fortunately, amidst all the news of murdered and kidnapped Jews, he has a house full of living Jews—vibrant survivors and young people who also carry his story.

Some nights, the footsteps are loud. Guests noisily open doors, repack their suitcases at 1 a.m. after a night in Shibuya. We chat over coffee, popcorn, sushi, or wine. My kids and I fix up the snack area, deliver towels or more Polaroid film.

On the shoe cabinets, the fridge, and now the microwave, are colorful stickers, each one featuring a gorgeous young face. The best friends and siblings of our friends. Heroes who fell in Gaza. Childhood friends murdered at the festival. Friends whose funerals they attended by the tens and twenties.

As soon as we saw the picture on the morning of May 22—beneath the headline, “Israeli embassy staff shooting”—we recognized his smile, his eye crinkles, his light. We knew it was him. A Jew who wanted to bring about peace through diplomacy—felled by terrorism in my own USA.

Yaron Lischinsky was the very first of our guests. He inspired us to *do this*—to create this community. He was our first Israeli—our first beam of light in Japan.
